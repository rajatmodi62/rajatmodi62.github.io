Today, we shall be considering the physical matters of symmetry. It is often overlooked, and instead substituted for by bruteforcing a transformer. The laws of symmetry have a way of hiding themselves in the dark: only careful controlled experiments reveal them, and how it effects the neural architectures in most curious of ways. Only in the eerie hours of the mourning, when the world is still asleep, can one truly appreciate it.

Thus, we will be learning about a paper called `Deep Sets` by Zaheer, and Russ. It is a very old paper. Yet, it reveals some useful insights and deeper connections to our search for the glom architecture. The purpose of this post is to merely enumerate a few of them. Only when a man understands them, can he hope to build upon them. 

Let us begin with a task: suppose we are given three numbers (1,2,3). We describe this as a set. What this means is that the order of the numbers does not matter. Thus, (1,2,3) is the same as (3,2,1). This is a very simple example of a set. In total, there are 3! = 6 permutations. 

Now, let us assume that we want to learn a neural net which is able to perform two kinds of operations on these sets:

- Invariance: No matter the order the numbers, it will always output the same answer. 
- Equivariance: If we permute the numbers, the output is also permuted in the same way. 

First, a key q is: why sets?

The answer is that you can break machine learning problems into sets: say your image has object 1, object 2.. object n, and you want to detect them. The order of objects does not matter, and thus you can treat them as a set. Here you want an `invariance` property. 

On the other hand, you can give the following argument: `humans look at the world by tracing their eyes in predictable trajectories`, for eg, when looking at another human, they will look at his face first, then his upper torso, then his legs. If this is the goal, then given an image, a neural net must be capable of decoding the different parts of the scene in a correct order.
