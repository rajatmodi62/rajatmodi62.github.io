Let us begin with a task: suppose we are given three numbers (1,2,3). We describe this as a set. What this means is that the order of the numbers does not matter. Thus, (1,2,3) is the same as (3,2,1). This is a very simple example of a set. In total, there are 3! = 6 permutations. 

Now, let us assume that we want to learn a neural net which is able to perform two kinds of operations on these sets:

- Invariance: No matter the order the numbers, it will always output the same answer. 
- Equivariance: If we permute the numbers, the output is also permuted in the same way. 

First, a key q is: why sets?

The answer is that you can break machine learning problems into sets: say your image has object 1, object 2.. object n, and you want to detect them. The order of objects does not matter, and thus you can treat them as a set. Here you want an `invariance` property. 

On the other hand, you can give the following argument: `humans look at the world by tracing their eyes in predictable trajectories`, for eg, when looking at another human, they will look at his face first, then his upper torso, then his legs. If this is the goal, then given an image, a neural net must be capable of decoding the different parts of the scene in a correct order. 

Let us try to probe deeper.

Next, you shuffle two elements of the set (2 4 3), and feed them again to the net. You get a new answer. Let us call it ans2. The constraint we wish to impose is that ans1=ans2. So, the algorithm is: take a set, shuffle it, enforce consistency among the outputs. What is the issue with this approach?

- Number of neurons in the first layer are fixed. This means that if size of the set is greater than no of neurons, it wont work 
- For a set of size n, there are n! permutations. This means that the number of shuffles required to enforce consistency is factorial in the size of the set. This means that training for a long time will be required. 

It turns out the intuitive answer is wrong. So, now let us look at a non-intuitive answer.

It is possible to break your neural net into two pieces called `neural net 1` and `neural net 2`. The mechanism of operation is a bit different:

- For every element of the set, you feed it `independently` to a neural net 1. This gives you a lot of answers. 
- You then sum all the answers of neural net 1. 
- You feed this sum to neural net 2. This gives you the final answer. 
- You train the two neural nets together. 

The advantages one gains is that: 

- Neural net 1 is `shared` across different elements of the set. 
- The number of neurons in first layer of neural net 1 no longer depends on number of elements in this set. It depends on the dimensionality of `each element of the set`. For our case, this dimensionality is 1. 

The disadvantages are:
- You have to store all answers of neural net 1 in memory. This means if set is large, the memory cache grows linearly. 
- One could get away with sort of cache pooling or maintaing a running sum of the answers, but that is just a clever hack. The actual mechanism is however subtle. 


The architecture of neural net 1, and neural net 2 is of `no consequence` other than boosting performance. The claim is that the way the information is processed is more important than the architecture. Thus, the matter at hand is not the architecture, but the deeper principles of how computation is `organized` in a neural net.

First, to see that the claim holds, let us consider an example. 

I will first describe a task,  show a baseline (intuitive) solution fail, and then show the above kind of kolmogorov solution succeed. Finally, we will chat a little bit about the connections of this approach to hinton's glom.

# <span style="font-size: 1.5rem; color: var(--border-header-bottom);">Predict variance of population of a set</span>

Suppose i give you a set (1,2,3). And i ask you to predict the variance of this set. The variance of this particular case is $\frac{2}{3}$. Here is where it gets tricky: suppose you take the set, and feed it into a neural net 1. The output of the neural net is `summed` which destroys the entire information of the set. Put simply, 1+2+3 = 6. By just looking at 6, it is impossible to estimate the variance of $\frac{2}{3}$. The reason is that 6 could also be made up by summing the elements of an alternate set say (2,2,2). The variance of this set is 0. 

Thus, the addition operation is not enough to capture the information of the set. So the key idea is: `let the output of neural net 1 be a vector instead of a scalar`. This way, the bottleneck (the sum) can learn to distinguish between different sets which could add to same sum. 

Now, suppose this net is learnt. How do we test it?

- To check the `generalization` of the network, we could start off with a sum (say 6). Then we feed it sets which it has not seen before. So, it should still correctly predict the variance of the set. 
- We could check the `invariance` property: shuffle the elements of the set, and check if the output is still the same. 

You do this enough number of times, and you will see that the network has learnt the invariance property. 

Now, let us get down to the actual code. Otherwise, it is just a lot of hand waving and nothing concrete.

I am going to define a few utitlities for training our little net. It just creates a dataset, estimates its variance, and trains the net w/ regression. 

```python 
# generate n sets of different sizes in size_range, and learn to predict their variance. 
def make_dataset(n_sets, size_range=(5, 10), val_range=(-3, 3)):
    """Generate `n_sets` sets of random size in `size_range`, each a bag of
    reals in `val_range`. Target = population variance of the set."""
    Xs, ys = [], []
    for _ in range(n_sets):
        n = np.random.randint(size_range[0], size_range[1] + 1)
        x = np.random.uniform(val_range[0], val_range[1], size=n).astype(np.float32)
        y = np.var(x).astype(np.float32)
        Xs.append(torch.tensor(x).unsqueeze(-1))  # shape (n, 1)
        ys.append(torch.tensor(y))
    return Xs, torch.stack(ys)

X_train, y_train = make_dataset(1500, (5, 10))
X_val, y_val = make_dataset(300, (5, 10))

print(f"{len(X_train)} training sets, sizes range from "
      f"{min(x.shape[0] for x in X_train)} to {max(x.shape[0] for x in X_train)} elements")
print("example set shape:", X_train[0].shape, " target (variance):", y_train[0].item())
```

# <span style="font-size: 1.5rem; color: var(--border-header-bottom);">Better case: feeding each part of a set one at a time</span>

Time to dump a little more code: 

```python 
import numpy as np
import torch
import torch.nn as nn
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

# -----------------------------------------------------------------------------
# Device Configuration
# -----------------------------------------------------------------------------
device = torch.device('cuda' if torch.cuda.is_available() else 'cpu')
print(f"Using device: {device}")

# -----------------------------------------------------------------------------
# DeepSets Architecture
# -----------------------------------------------------------------------------
class DeepSets(nn.Module):
    """phi -> sum-pool -> rho, i.e. f(X) = rho( sum_{x in X} phi(x) )  [Theorem 2 / Sec. 3.1]"""
    def __init__(self, latent_dim=16, pool='sum'):
        super().__init__()
        self.phi = nn.Sequential(          # applied to each set element independently
            nn.Linear(1, 32), nn.ReLU(),
            nn.Linear(32, latent_dim),
        )
        self.rho = nn.Sequential(          # applied once, to the pooled representation
            nn.Linear(latent_dim, 32), nn.ReLU(),
            nn.Linear(32, 1),
        )
        self.pool = pool

    def forward(self, x):
        # x: (n_elements, 1) -- a set of n real numbers, n can vary between calls
        phi_x = self.phi(x)                  # (n_elements, latent_dim), SAME phi for every element
        if self.pool == 'sum':
            pooled = phi_x.sum(dim=0)         # <-- the sum from Theorem 2
        elif self.pool == 'mean':
            pooled = phi_x.mean(dim=0)        # also permutation invariant
        elif self.pool == 'max':
            pooled = phi_x.max(dim=0).values  # also permutation invariant
        else:
            raise ValueError(self.pool)
        return self.rho(pooled).squeeze(-1)   # scalar prediction

# -----------------------------------------------------------------------------
# Training & Evaluation Functions
# -----------------------------------------------------------------------------
def train(model, X, y, epochs=20, lr=1e-3, log_every=100):
    model.train()
    opt = torch.optim.Adam(model.parameters(), lr=lr)
    loss_fn = nn.MSELoss()
    history = []
    global_step = 0
    n_samples = len(X)

    for epoch in range(epochs):
        total_loss = 0.0
        order = np.random.permutation(n_samples)
        
        for i in order:
            global_step += 1
            # Push individual set tensor to GPU
            x_i = X[i].to(device)
            y_i = y[i].to(device)

            opt.zero_grad()
            pred = model(x_i)
            loss = loss_fn(pred, y_i)
            loss.backward()
            opt.step()

            loss_val = loss.item()
            total_loss += loss_val

            # Iteration-level logging
            if global_step % log_every == 0 or global_step == 1:
                print(f"Iter {global_step:5d} | Epoch {epoch+1:2d}/{epochs} | Step MSE: {loss_val:.4f}")

        epoch_mse = total_loss / n_samples
        history.append(epoch_mse)
        print(f"==> Epoch {epoch+1:2d} Complete | Avg Train MSE: {epoch_mse:.4f}\n")

    return history

def evaluate(model, X, y):
    model.eval()
    loss_fn = nn.MSELoss()
    with torch.no_grad():
        preds = torch.stack([model(x.to(device)) for x in X])
        y_dev = y.to(device)
        return loss_fn(preds, y_dev).item()

# -----------------------------------------------------------------------------
# Execution & Plotting
# -----------------------------------------------------------------------------
# Move model to GPU
deep_sets = DeepSets(latent_dim=16, pool='sum').to(device)

# Train with logs every 100 iterations
history = train(deep_sets, X_train, y_train, epochs=20, log_every=100)

# Evaluate on Validation Set
val_mse = evaluate(deep_sets, X_val, y_val)
print(f"Validation MSE: {val_mse:.4f}  (target variance ranges roughly 0-3)")

# Plot Training Curve
plt.figure(figsize=(5, 3))
plt.plot(history, marker='o')
plt.xlabel("Epoch")
plt.ylabel("Train MSE")
plt.title("Deep Sets Training Curve")
plt.grid(True, alpha=0.3)
plt.tight_layout()
plt.show()
```

# <span style="font-size: 1.5rem; color: var(--border-header-bottom);">Encoding equivariance in the net</span>



We begin by defining two layers containing three neurons each. Please dont ask me why i didnt take a 150 layer neural net with 1000's of neurons each. I will just roll my eyes.

The first layer is a `clamp` layer, and the second layer is the `output` layer. I am not introducing any other layers here because gradient descent messes stuff up. I don't like gradient descent. The first layer is given $x_1, x_2, x_3$ as input. The output of the second layer is $y_1, y_2, y_3$.

It is said to be `perfectly equivariant` if the output changes in the same way as the input. Till now, i hope we are on the same page. Now, it is time to give our little neural net some weights.

So, the little dude has many connections. On each connection, there is a little squiggly weight. There are 3 neurons in first layer, 3 neurons in second, so 3*3 = 9 weights. You can pack them into something called a matrix. So, the weight matrix W is given by: 

$$\mathbf{W} = \begin{bmatrix} w_{11} & w_{12} & w_{13} \\ w_{21} & w_{22} & w_{23} \\ w_{31} & w_{32} & w_{33} \end{bmatrix}$$

These are 9 numbers, which can have any value. Thus, the degree of freedom of this matrix is 9.

The question we wish to ask: `is there any weight matrix which can GUARANTEE PERFECT EQUIVARIANCE?`. If so, what is it?

Now, our (dear) shinchan will pull a little matrix up from his ass (courtesy of the mathematicians). The special form is given by:


$$\mathbf{W}_{\text{equivariant}} = \begin{bmatrix} \lambda & \gamma & \gamma \\ \gamma & \lambda & \gamma \\ \gamma & \gamma & \lambda \end{bmatrix} $$

Don't ask me where it came from. It does not matter. Only two things matter:
- It exists 
- It has only two degrees of freedom: $\lambda$ and $\gamma$.

Now, my claim is this matrix will guarantee perfect equivariance. Let us see how. 

Suppose you multiply $[x_1, x_2, x_3]^T$ by this matrix. You  get:

$$\mathbf{W}_{\text{equivariant}} \begin{bmatrix} x_1 \\ x_2 \\ x_3 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} \lambda x_1 + \gamma x_2 + \gamma x_3 \\ \gamma x_1 + \lambda x_2 + \gamma x_3 \\ \gamma x_1 + \gamma x_2 + \lambda x_3 \end{bmatrix}$$

Now, consider the second case when u shuffle the dude. Here you end up multiplying $[x_1, x_3, x_2]^T$ by the same matrix. You get:

$$\mathbf{W}_{\text{equivariant}} \begin{bmatrix} x_1 \\ x_3 \\ x_2 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} \lambda x_1 + \gamma x_3 + \gamma x_2 \\ \gamma x_1 + \lambda x_3 + \gamma x_2 \\ \gamma x_1 + \gamma x_3 + \lambda x_2 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} \lambda x_1 + \gamma x_2 + \gamma x_3 \\ \gamma x_1 + \gamma x_2 + \lambda x_3 \\\gamma x_1 + \lambda x_2 + \gamma x_3   \end{bmatrix}$$

Now, look carefully at the two output matrix in isolation:

$$\begin{bmatrix} 
\lambda x_1 + \gamma x_2 + \gamma x_3 \\ 
\color{red}{\gamma x_1 + \lambda x_2 + \gamma x_3} \\ 
\color{green}{\gamma x_1 + \gamma x_2 + \lambda x_3} 
\end{bmatrix}$$

$$\begin{bmatrix} 
\lambda x_1 + \gamma x_2 + \gamma x_3 \\ 
\color{green}{\gamma x_1 + \gamma x_2 + \lambda x_3} \\ 
\color{red}{\gamma x_1 + \lambda x_2 + \gamma x_3} 
\end{bmatrix}$$


You can output the second matrix from the first by simply swapping the red and green rows. This is exactly what we wanted to show. Thus, this matrix guarantees perfect equivariance. This is single layer of our neural net. Now, imagine there are many such layers stacked on top of each another. The claim is that the entire net will be perfectly equivariant. And it is easy to see why: if each layer is perfectly equivariant, then the entire net is perfectly equivariant. 

How do we test the `equivariance property`  of such a net?

- Take a set, feed it to the net, and get an output. Lets call it ans1. 
- Now, PERMUTE your set in some order, feed it to the net, and get an output. Lets call it ans2.
- Apply the same permutation to ans1, and check if it is equal to ans2. If it is, then the net is perfectly equivariant. 

My claim is that the outputs will be identical, and the difference will be a `perfect zero`. Perfect zero is a nasty business. People think that it is not possible, because deep neural nets are inherently stochastic. That is sooooo wrong. It is easy to show this in code. And, it is time to do that. 

```python 
import torch
import torch.nn as nn

torch.manual_seed(0)


class EquivariantLayerExact(nn.Module):
    """Same as EquivariantLayer, but the pooled summary is computed in a
    CANONICAL (sorted) order, so the floating-point additions are bit-for-bit
    identical regardless of what order the elements arrived in."""
    def __init__(self, in_dim=1, out_dim=8):
        super().__init__()
        self.lam = nn.Linear(in_dim, out_dim, bias=True)    # per-element term
        self.gam = nn.Linear(in_dim, out_dim, bias=False)  # applied to the canonically-pooled summary

    def forward(self, x):                       # x: (n_elements, in_dim)
        # sort rows by a value-only key: any permutation of the same rows
        # sorts to the identical sequence, fixing the summation order
        keys = x.sum(dim=-1)
        order = torch.argsort(keys, stable=True)
        x_canonical = x[order]  # rmodi: creating summary vector needs outputs summed in identical order
        s = x_canonical.sum(dim=0, keepdim=True)  # bit-identical for any permutation of x
        return self.lam(x) + self.gam(s)          # self.lam(x) needs no fix: per-row, order-independent


class EquivariantNetExact(nn.Module):
    def __init__(self, hidden=32):
        super().__init__()
        self.l1 = EquivariantLayerExact(1, hidden)
        self.l2 = EquivariantLayerExact(hidden, hidden)
        self.l3 = EquivariantLayerExact(hidden, 1)

    def forward(self, x):
        x = torch.relu(self.l1(x))
        x = torch.relu(self.l2(x))
        return self.l3(x).squeeze(-1)


# ---- test: bit-for-bit equivariance across several random permutations ----
net = EquivariantNetExact()
x = torch.randn(6, 1)

print("testing exact equivariance across 5 random permutations:\n")
for trial in range(5):
    perm = torch.randperm(6)
    out_apply_then_permute = net(x)[perm]
    out_permute_then_apply = net(x[perm])

    diff = (out_apply_then_permute - out_permute_then_apply).abs().max().item()
    exact = torch.equal(out_apply_then_permute, out_permute_then_apply)
    print(f"trial {trial}:  max |diff| = {diff}   bit-for-bit identical: {exact}")

assert all(
    torch.equal(net(x)[p], net(x[p]))
    for p in (torch.randperm(6) for _ in range(20))
), "equivariance violated"
print("\nPASSED: exactly equivariant (diff == 0.0) across 20 random permutations")
```

```python
trial 3:  max |diff| = 0.0   bit-for-bit identical: True
```

Now, i would like to talk about a little something called the `arnold-kolmogorov superposition theoram`. First, let us recall what kolmogorov theoram looks like: 

<div style="background-color: #ffebee; border-left: 5px solid #d32f2f; border-radius: 4px; padding: 16px; margin: 16px 0; color: #212121; font-family: sans-serif; line-height: 1.6;">

<p><b>Theorem 8 (Kolmogorov–Arnold representation)</b> <i>Let $f : [0, 1]^M \to \mathbb{R}$ be an arbitrary multivariate continuous function iff it has the representation</i></p>

$$f(x_1, \dots, x_M) = \rho \left( \sum_{m=1}^M \lambda_m \phi(x_m) \right)$$

<p><i>with continuous outer and inner functions $\rho : \mathbb{R}^{2M+1} \to \mathbb{R}$ and $\phi : \mathbb{R} \to \mathbb{R}^{2M+1}$. The inner function $\phi$ is independent of the function $f$.</i></p>

</div>

Let me break this for you. It says you start with a vector x as input. In your case, x can be an image, or a set. All you see are pixels, but in fact it is a signal. Image is a signal, and a signal is a function. And functions are continous. Which means that image is continous (percept is a field).

# <span style="font-size: 1.5rem; color: var(--border-header-bottom);">The bias blunder</span>

Around 1980's, cognitive scientists got a curious idea: it is possible to feed inputs to a neuron, and then add the input. It was thought that adding an additional parameter (called bias) was a good idea to control it's sensitivity. We must teach ourselves what we mean by sensitivity. 

Say your neuron fires when the `total` input is greater than 0.9. Now, consider this:

- The sum of input along the incoming synapses is merely 0.1. 
- The sum of input along the incoming synapses is 0.8. 

For the first case of 0.1, the neuron wont fire. To drive the total input to 0.9, all the input connections will have to work really hard to send an additional value of 0.8 to the incoming neuron. This is a lot of work, which means that the input connections will get tired. It makes a sense to introduce a tradeoff, say you add a bias of 0.4. Now, the input connections only have to increase their input by 0.8-0.4 = 0.4 times. This is a lot less work, and the input connections will be happy. A larger value of bias thus means that the neuron is more sensitive. If bias is 0.85, if total input is 0.05, neuron will fire. It is `easier' for inputs to sum up to 0.05, which means that this neuron will be ON most of the time. For computing machines which want to be energy efficient, the bias might need to be a little bit high, so that the incoming connections do less work, thereby consuming less energy lol. A digital machine does not have an issue of fatigue, but an analog machine which slides itself in the continous energy landscape does. The time it takes to slide from one point to another is proportional to the distance between them, so a larger bias means that the machine will not need to slide as much, and thus shall be more energy efficient. 

And thus people got a curious idea: why not add bias to input locations itself? And that turned out to be a very bad idea. Let us see why.

Let us now turn our attention to this matter of degrees of freedom. Recall the matrix we learnt earlier from the deepset paper. It looks like:
$$\mathbf{W}_{\text{equivariant}} = \begin{bmatrix} \lambda & \gamma & \gamma \\ \gamma & \lambda & \gamma \\ \gamma & \gamma & \lambda \end{bmatrix} $$

We can see this has 9 values but only 2 degrees of freedom. This suggests that it should be possible to build a sort of outer loop where the neural net can choose how much degrees of freedom it ought to possesss. Therefore, it might be interesting to imagine learning in two phases:

- first, an input vector is presented. 
- next, the neural net chooses how many degree of freedom each layer ought to possess. 
- it clamps its own weight matrices. 
- a forward pass is performed, and the training proceeds. 
- at no step of the training, do we tell it what dof it oughts to have. 

The idea of reducing the degree of freedom in the net's own weight matrix is somewhat different from the mixture of experts. In MOE the weights are separate for each expert, whereas here the weights are shared. It is possible to reduce the freedom by choosing to trim the weights artificially, or learning a low rank projection. However, a loop where the weights just reduce themselves if so desired might be a more desirable solution.

Now that we have had our lunch, let us continue. We now consider this matter of attention based nets like transformers (particularly set transformer). The reason for using attention is that it is indeed a lot more powerful than mere usage of MLPs. The behaviour we desire of it is 
the one which enforces a perfect equivariance, and perfect invariance. By `perfect` we mean 0 bitwise errors on fluctuations of the input. 

Consider a query q operating on several keys $k_1, k_2, k_3$. Our query computes a dot product with each of the keys, and applies softmax, and finally obtains attention weights $\alpha_1, \alpha_2, \alpha_3$. The output is then given by $\alpha_1 v_1 + \alpha_2 v_2 + \alpha_3 v_3$. This is obvious. However, we must discuss the messiness of real world, i.e. a digital machine. 

In mathematics, one is taught A + (B + C) = (A + B) + C. However, in a digital machine, this is not true. The reason is that the addition is not exact, and there are rounding errors. Thus, if you add A + (B + C), you will get a different answer than (A + B) + C. This is a problem because it means that if you `fluctuate` the order of keys, the output will also fluctuate. Therefore, you need a mechanism which always performs the summation in the same order, regardless of the order of the keys. This can be merely achieved by sorting the $\alpha_i k_i$ in a canonical order, then performing the summation. 

One is indeed tempted to sweep this under the rug, and train a transformer at scale, in the hope that the network's weights will `absorb` the resultant errors. However, this is not a good idea, for we are sending a wrong signal to the memory. The available memory is finite, and it would have been far better to handle such issues by merely designing the architecture correctly. 

Now, let us again consider the operation $v_{out} = \alpha_1 v_1 + \alpha_2 v_2 + \alpha_3 v_3$, where v is the `value` vector obtained post-attention for our given query q. If there are multiple queries $[q_1, q_2, q_3]$ stacked in parallel, the resultant value vector will be $[v_{out,1}, v_{out,2}, v_{out,3}]$. If we swap the positions of q_1, q_2, the resultant query comes $[q_2, q_1, q_3]$, and the resultant value vector will be $[v_{out,2}, v_{out,1}, v_{out,3}]$. The value vector is thus equivariant to the query vector. 

In the case of self-attention, the query/key/value vectors are derived from the input sequence by `weight sharing` 3 separate MLPs across all input tokens. This means that each token is processed `independently` by the same MLPs, before the actual attention operation is applied. This means that the estimate of k, q, v will also be equivariant w.r.t the input sequence. Thus, the self attention operation is equivariant w.r.t. the input sequence. And that is what we mean by the SAB block above. 

It is possible to show the bitwise zero equivariance of the SAB block by the means of following code:
```python 
import math
import torch
import torch.nn as nn

# ============================================================
# MABExact — Multihead Attention Block, bit-exact under permutation.
# X = queries, Y = keys/values.
# The key/value rows are sorted into a CANONICAL (value-based) order
# before the attention sum happens, so softmax's internal sum and
# attn @ V's weighted sum always add the same terms in the same
# sequence -- eliminating floating-point non-associativity, not just
# shrinking it. Query order is untouched.
# ============================================================
class MABExact(nn.Module):
    def __init__(self, dim, n_heads=4):
        super().__init__()
        assert dim % n_heads == 0
        self.n_heads = n_heads
        self.d_head = dim // n_heads
        self.Wq = nn.Linear(dim, dim)
        self.Wk = nn.Linear(dim, dim)
        self.Wv = nn.Linear(dim, dim)
        self.Wo = nn.Linear(dim, dim)
        self.ln1 = nn.LayerNorm(dim)
        self.rff = nn.Sequential(nn.Linear(dim, dim), nn.ReLU(), nn.Linear(dim, dim))
        self.ln2 = nn.LayerNorm(dim)

    def forward(self, X, Y):
        # canonicalize Y's row order BEFORE computing K, V
        keys = Y.sum(dim=-1)
        order = torch.argsort(keys, stable=True)
        Y_canon = Y[order]

        Q, K, V = self.Wq(X), self.Wk(Y_canon), self.Wv(Y_canon)
        n, m, d = Q.shape[0], K.shape[0], Q.shape[1]
        h, dh = self.n_heads, self.d_head
        Qh = Q.view(n, h, dh).transpose(0, 1)
        Kh = K.view(m, h, dh).transpose(0, 1)
        Vh = V.view(m, h, dh).transpose(0, 1)
        attn = torch.softmax(Qh @ Kh.transpose(1, 2) / math.sqrt(dh), dim=-1)  # canonical-order sum
        O = (attn @ Vh).transpose(0, 1).reshape(n, d)                          # canonical-order sum
        O = self.Wo(O)
        H = self.ln1(X + O)
        return self.ln2(H + self.rff(H))


class SABExact(nn.Module):
    def __init__(self, dim, n_heads=4):
        super().__init__()
        self.mab = MABExact(dim, n_heads)

    def forward(self, X):
        return self.mab(X, X)


# ------------------------------------------------------------
# PROOF: SAB is EXACTLY equivariant -- bit-for-bit, not "close to"
# ------------------------------------------------------------
torch.manual_seed(0)
sab = SABExact(dim=8, n_heads=2)
X = torch.randn(6, 8)
perm = torch.randperm(6)

out1 = sab(X)[perm]
out2 = sab(X[perm])
print("SAB equivariance check")
print("  max diff =", (out1 - out2).abs().max().item())
print("  torch.equal (bit-for-bit identical) =", torch.equal(out1, out2))
assert torch.equal(out1, out2), "SAB is NOT bit-exact equivariant!"
print("  PASSED -- exactly equivariant.\n")
```

Next, we wish to talk about an architectural block called the MAB. Consider it carefully. First, we are given an input sequence Y. There are some `summary queries` which are learnt in the X vector. First, the input sequence Y undergoes a sort of non-linear transformation along each of the tokens `independently`. Finally, the summary queries in X are doing cross attention. This is an obvious point. 

A not so obvious point is the nature of symmetry this model invokes: Y is invariant. What we mean is that key/value computed by the y's tokens are eventually summed up, therefore there is no sense of ordering. Next, consider X. Those are the summary queries. They are equivariant, for each query is independent of the other queries. The final residual connection, and the FF network is operating independently on the individual queries, which makes the final model equivariant. The key takeaway is that this design allows the model to retain both invariance on input Y, and equivariance on summary vectors X. It is possible to check this fact by the following code:

```python

import math
import torch
import torch.nn as nn

# ============================================================
# MABExact — Multihead Attention Block, bit-exact under permutation.
# X = queries, Y = keys/values.
# The key/value rows are sorted into a CANONICAL (value-based) order
# before the attention sum happens, so softmax's internal sum and
# attn @ V's weighted sum always add the same terms in the same
# sequence -- eliminating floating-point non-associativity, not just
# shrinking it. Query order is untouched.
# ============================================================
class MABExact(nn.Module):
    def __init__(self, dim, n_heads=4):
        super().__init__()
        assert dim % n_heads == 0
        self.n_heads = n_heads
        self.d_head = dim // n_heads
        self.Wq = nn.Linear(dim, dim)
        self.Wk = nn.Linear(dim, dim)
        self.Wv = nn.Linear(dim, dim)
        self.Wo = nn.Linear(dim, dim)
        self.ln1 = nn.LayerNorm(dim)
        self.rff = nn.Sequential(nn.Linear(dim, dim), nn.ReLU(), nn.Linear(dim, dim))
        self.ln2 = nn.LayerNorm(dim)

    def forward(self, X, Y):
        # canonicalize Y's row order BEFORE computing K, V -- any permutation of the
        # same multiset of rows sorts to the identical sequence
        keys = Y.sum(dim=-1)
        order = torch.argsort(keys, stable=True)
        Y_canon = Y[order]

        Q, K, V = self.Wq(X), self.Wk(Y_canon), self.Wv(Y_canon)
        n, m, d = Q.shape[0], K.shape[0], Q.shape[1]
        h, dh = self.n_heads, self.d_head
        Qh = Q.view(n, h, dh).transpose(0, 1)
        Kh = K.view(m, h, dh).transpose(0, 1)
        Vh = V.view(m, h, dh).transpose(0, 1)
        attn = torch.softmax(Qh @ Kh.transpose(1, 2) / math.sqrt(dh), dim=-1)  # canonical-order sum
        O = (attn @ Vh).transpose(0, 1).reshape(n, d)                          # canonical-order sum
        O = self.Wo(O)
        H = self.ln1(X + O)
        return self.ln2(H + self.rff(H))


# ------------------------------------------------------------
# PROOF 1: MAB is EXACTLY equivariant in X -- bit-for-bit
# ------------------------------------------------------------
torch.manual_seed(0)
mab = MABExact(dim=8, n_heads=2)
X = torch.randn(6, 8)   # queries
Y = torch.randn(4, 8)   # keys/values, deliberately a different size

perm_x = torch.randperm(6)
out1 = mab(X, Y)[perm_x]
out2 = mab(X[perm_x], Y)
print("MAB equivariance-in-X check")
print("  max diff =", (out1 - out2).abs().max().item(), " bit-exact:", torch.equal(out1, out2))
assert torch.equal(out1, out2), "MAB is NOT bit-exact equivariant in X!"
print("  PASSED\n")

# ------------------------------------------------------------
# PROOF 2: MAB is EXACTLY invariant to Y -- bit-for-bit
# ------------------------------------------------------------

out3 = mab(X, Y)
out4 = mab(X, Y[perm_y])
print("MAB invariance-in-Y check")
print("  max diff =", (out3 - out4).abs().max().item(), " bit-exact:", torch.equal(out3, out4))

print("  PASSED")
```

It is helpful to think of ISAB block as a cascade of two blocks. First, the input sequence X is fed in, and K summary vectors (in sequence I) are learnt via cross-attention. The output is K summary vectors (or the so called values lol). Next, there is a curios trick i like to call as the `slot exchange`. 

If you look carefully, the summary vector goes as the input sequence to the second block, and X is fed as the summary vector. Why is it so? This is because, in the first block, the summary vectors are `learned` from the data. The information about relative orderings of X are destroyed. To compensate for the loss of information, the input seequence X is assumed as a `summary` vector in the second block. Since individual tokens of X are now equivariant queries, they regain their ordering information. The issue however is: `the first block collapsed information about ordering of X into I, and then X is kept as a summary vector in the second block to regain the lost information`. However, this relies on the assumption that the network is capable to do it. This point is subtle, and  for now, we will leave it to the reader to ponder on it.

Together these two blocks form the ISAB block. From outside, a sequence X is fed in, and a sequence X is obtained as output without a loss of spatial resolution (aka the length of the sequence X). Thus, we can stack many such ISAB blocks together to form a deep network and then train them w/ gradient descent + backpropagation. And this is called a set transformer. 

Now, it is time to make it perform a trivial task. It is so simple, that most people will say it is useless. They would rather focus on training it on a large dataset, and get sota. But, the inner nature of things reveals themselves in the simplest of settings, for it allows a man to isolate individual variables whose combined effects are otherwise too entangled to understand. 

The task we wish to pick up is called amortized clustering. Please allow  me to explain it first.

On the left, we are given a bunch of points in 2D space. The points are `generated` from a structure. As such, they form distinct clusters. Now look at the right figure. It is possible to `pack` all these points into different clusters. Each of the clusters is colored differently, like `red, light blue, or dark blue`. A cluser is said to be `good` if it is very compact, and is able to surround the points in a tight manner. The key point being that : `every point is a part of some cluster`. Now, let us look at each cluster in isolation. 

You see, for each cluster, there are several parameters we must predict. For eg, where is the center of cluster located? This would have two coordinate <x,y>. Next, what is the spread of this cluster? It will have a standard deviation or radius of sorts like $\sigma_1,\sigma_2$. 

Next consider the following task:
- You are given a bunch of such points. 
- You are asked to predict the parameters of the clusters.

A normal model like k-means or EM algorithm would be iterative: it will run for several iterations for each cluster. THis means that it takes a long time to converge. However, we are interested in learning a model which works in a `single forward pass`: Give it a bunch of points, and it will predict the parameters of the clusters. This is called amortized clustering. The `hope is ` that the learnt model will be faster than EM, and more accurate. First, it is helpful to realize what exactly are we trying to predict. The  nature of input is clear: it is N points, each being a 2D value (x, y). Lets take a look at what the model should predict lol.

So it predicts mean, std, and logits for each cluster. Basically, in the logits are then run through softmax, and model is made to predict how confident it is that each cluster exists. Now, it is time to train the entire set transformer on this task.

```python
import math
import time
import numpy as np
import torch
import torch.nn as nn
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

torch.manual_seed(0)
np.random.seed(0)

# ============================================================
# Attention primitives: MultiheadAttention -> MAB -> SAB / PMA
# All batched: (B, n, dim)
# ============================================================
class MultiheadAttention(nn.Module):
    def __init__(self, dim, n_heads):
        super().__init__()
        assert dim % n_heads == 0
        self.n_heads = n_heads
        self.d_head = dim // n_heads
        self.Wq = nn.Linear(dim, dim)
        self.Wk = nn.Linear(dim, dim)
        self.Wv = nn.Linear(dim, dim)
        self.Wo = nn.Linear(dim, dim)

    def forward(self, X, Y):
        Q, K, V = self.Wq(X), self.Wk(Y), self.Wv(Y)
        B, n, d = Q.shape
        m = K.shape[1]
        h, dh = self.n_heads, self.d_head
        Qh = Q.view(B, n, h, dh).transpose(1, 2)
        Kh = K.view(B, m, h, dh).transpose(1, 2)
        Vh = V.view(B, m, h, dh).transpose(1, 2)
        attn = torch.softmax(Qh @ Kh.transpose(-2, -1) / math.sqrt(dh), dim=-1)
        O = (attn @ Vh).transpose(1, 2).reshape(B, n, d)
        return self.Wo(O)


class MAB(nn.Module):
    """MAB(X,Y): equivariant in X, invariant in Y."""
    def __init__(self, dim, n_heads):
        super().__init__()
        self.mha = MultiheadAttention(dim, n_heads)
        self.ln1 = nn.LayerNorm(dim)
        self.rff = nn.Sequential(nn.Linear(dim, dim), nn.ReLU(), nn.Linear(dim, dim))
        self.ln2 = nn.LayerNorm(dim)

    def forward(self, X, Y):
        H = self.ln1(X + self.mha(X, Y))
        return self.ln2(H + self.rff(H))


class SAB(nn.Module):
    """SAB(X) := MAB(X, X) -- equivariant."""
    def __init__(self, dim, n_heads):
        super().__init__()
        self.mab = MAB(dim, n_heads)

    def forward(self, X):
        return self.mab(X, X)


class PMA(nn.Module):
    """PMA_k(Z) := MAB(S, rFF(Z)) -- invariant, k trainable seed vectors."""
    def __init__(self, dim, k_seeds, n_heads):
        super().__init__()
        self.S = nn.Parameter(torch.randn(1, k_seeds, dim))
        self.rff = nn.Sequential(nn.Linear(dim, dim), nn.ReLU(), nn.Linear(dim, dim))
        self.mab = MAB(dim, n_heads)

    def forward(self, Z):
        B = Z.shape[0]
        S = self.S.expand(B, -1, -1)
        return self.mab(S, self.rff(Z))


# ============================================================
# The two models being compared
# ============================================================
class AmortizedClusterer(nn.Module):
    """SAB encoder + PMA(k=K) decoder -> per-cluster (mean, log_std, mixing logit)."""
    def __init__(self, K=4, hidden=48, n_heads=4):
        super().__init__()
        self.K = K
        self.proj = nn.Linear(2, hidden)
        self.sab1 = SAB(hidden, n_heads)
        self.sab2 = SAB(hidden, n_heads)
        self.pma = PMA(hidden, K, n_heads)
        self.head = nn.Sequential(nn.Linear(hidden, hidden), nn.ReLU(), nn.Linear(hidden, 5))

    def forward(self, X):
        Z = self.proj(X)
        Z = self.sab1(Z)
        Z = self.sab2(Z)
        clusters = self.pma(Z)                 # (B, K, hidden)
        out = self.head(clusters)               # (B, K, 5)
        mean = out[..., 0:2]
        log_std = out[..., 2:4].clamp(-4, 2)
        log_weights = torch.log_softmax(out[..., 4], dim=-1)
        return mean, log_std, log_weights


class PoolingClusterer(nn.Module):
    """Deep-Sets-style baseline: independent per-point encoding + fixed mean-pooling."""
    def __init__(self, K=4, hidden=48):
        super().__init__()
        self.K = K
        self.phi = nn.Sequential(nn.Linear(2, hidden), nn.ReLU(),
                                  nn.Linear(hidden, hidden), nn.ReLU())
        self.rho = nn.Sequential(nn.Linear(hidden, hidden), nn.ReLU(), nn.Linear(hidden, K * 5))

    def forward(self, X):
        z = self.phi(X).mean(dim=1)             # fixed mean-pool, NO attention
        out = self.rho(z).view(-1, self.K, 5)
        mean = out[..., 0:2]
        log_std = out[..., 2:4].clamp(-4, 2)
        log_weights = torch.log_softmax(out[..., 4], dim=-1)
        return mean, log_std, log_weights


# ============================================================
# Loss: Gaussian-mixture negative log-likelihood
# ============================================================
def gmm_nll(X, mean, log_std, log_weights):
    Xe = X.unsqueeze(2); mu = mean.unsqueeze(1); ls = log_std.unsqueeze(1)
    std = ls.exp()
    log_norm = -0.5 * (((Xe - mu) / std) ** 2).sum(-1) - ls.sum(-1) - math.log(2 * math.pi)
    log_mix = log_weights.unsqueeze(1) + log_norm
    return -torch.logsumexp(log_mix, dim=-1).mean()


# ============================================================
# Data: a fresh random mixture every episode
# ============================================================
def sample_mixture_task(K=4, n_points=150, mean_range=4.0, std_range=(0.25, 0.6), rng=None):
    """One random 'episode': random cluster means, random per-axis stds, random
    (Dirichlet) mixing weights, then n_points sampled from that mixture."""
    if rng is None:
        rng = np.random.default_rng()
    means = rng.uniform(-mean_range, mean_range, size=(K, 2))
    stds = rng.uniform(std_range[0], std_range[1], size=(K, 2))
    weights = rng.dirichlet(alpha=np.ones(K) * 3.0)
    assignments = rng.choice(K, size=n_points, p=weights)
    points = means[assignments] + stds[assignments] * rng.normal(size=(n_points, 2))
    return points.astype(np.float32), means, stds, weights, assignments


def sample_batch(batch_size, n_points, rng):
    tasks = [sample_mixture_task(K=4, n_points=n_points, rng=rng) for _ in range(batch_size)]
    X = torch.tensor(np.stack([t[0] for t in tasks]))
    return X, tasks


# ============================================================
# Training loop
# ============================================================
def train_clusterer(model, n_steps, batch_size, n_points, rng, lr=1e-3):
    opt = torch.optim.Adam(model.parameters(), lr=lr)
    history = []
    for step in range(n_steps):
        X, _ = sample_batch(batch_size, n_points, rng)
        opt.zero_grad()
        mean, log_std, log_weights = model(X)
        loss = gmm_nll(X, mean, log_std, log_weights)
        loss.backward()
        opt.step()
        history.append(loss.item())
        if (step + 1) % 1500 == 0:
            print(f"  step {step+1:5d}   avg NLL (last 1500 steps): {np.mean(history[-1500:]):.4f}")
    return history


# ============================================================
# Run: train both models, plot the comparison
# ============================================================
print("training Set Transformer clusterer (SAB encoder + PMA decoder)...")
t0 = time.time()
st_model = AmortizedClusterer(K=4, hidden=48)
st_history = train_clusterer(st_model, n_steps=6000, batch_size=8, n_points=120,
                              rng=np.random.default_rng(0))
print(f"done in {time.time()-t0:.0f}s\n")

print("training no-attention pooling baseline (same objective, same budget)...")
t0 = time.time()
pool_model = PoolingClusterer(K=4, hidden=48)
pool_history = train_clusterer(pool_model, n_steps=6000, batch_size=8, n_points=120,
                                rng=np.random.default_rng(1))
print(f"done in {time.time()-t0:.0f}s")

plt.figure(figsize=(6, 3.5))
plt.plot(st_history, label="Set Transformer (SAB + PMA)", alpha=0.8)
plt.plot(pool_history, label="Pooling baseline (no attention)", alpha=0.8)
plt.xlabel("training step"); plt.ylabel("Gaussian-mixture NLL")
plt.legend(); plt.title("Training curves -- a NEW random mixture every step")
plt.tight_layout(); plt.show()
```

```python
# ============================================================
# CELL — compare Ground Truth vs. classical EM vs. Set Transformer,
# with cluster COLORS forced to match across rows (Hungarian matching
# on cluster-center distance -- same idea as DETR's bipartite matching,
# just matching clusters to clusters instead of boxes to boxes)
# ============================================================
from sklearn.mixture import GaussianMixture
from matplotlib.patches import Ellipse
from scipy.optimize import linear_sum_assignment

def match_to_reference(ref_means, means, stds, weights):
    """Find the assignment that best matches `means` to `ref_means`
    (minimizing total center distance), then reorder means/stds/weights
    so cluster k always corresponds to ref_means' cluster k."""
    cost = np.linalg.norm(ref_means[:, None, :] - means[None, :, :], axis=-1)  # (K, K)
    row_idx, col_idx = linear_sum_assignment(cost)
    order = col_idx[np.argsort(row_idx)]              # means[order[k]] best matches ref_means[k]
    return means[order], stds[order], weights[order]

def responsibilities(x, mean, std, weights):
    xe = x[:, None, :]; mu = mean[None, :, :]; s = std[None, :, :]
    log_norm = -0.5 * (((xe - mu) / s) ** 2).sum(-1) - np.log(s).sum(-1) - np.log(2 * np.pi)
    log_r = np.log(weights)[None, :] + log_norm
    log_r -= log_r.max(axis=1, keepdims=True)
    r = np.exp(log_r)
    return r / r.sum(axis=1, keepdims=True)

def plot_clustering(ax, x, mean, std, weights, colors):
    r = responsibilities(x, mean, std, weights)
    assign = r.argmax(axis=1)
    for k in range(mean.shape[0]):
        pts_k = x[assign == k]
        if len(pts_k) > 0:
            ax.scatter(pts_k[:, 0], pts_k[:, 1], s=14, color=colors[k % len(colors)],
                       edgecolors='black', linewidths=0.3)
        ell = Ellipse(mean[k], width=4 * std[k, 0], height=4 * std[k, 1],
                      fill=False, edgecolor=colors[k % len(colors)], linewidth=1.8)
        ax.add_patch(ell)
    ax.set_xticks([]); ax.set_yticks([])

colors = ['#33bbbb', '#e03131', '#6741d9', '#c9b458']

test_rng = np.random.default_rng(42)
n_test = 5
test_tasks = [sample_mixture_task(K=4, n_points=150, rng=test_rng) for _ in range(n_test)]
X_test = torch.tensor(np.stack([t[0] for t in test_tasks]))

with torch.no_grad():
    st_mean, st_log_std, st_log_w = st_model(X_test)

em_fits = []
for pts, *_ in test_tasks:
    gm = GaussianMixture(n_components=4, covariance_type='diag', n_init=3, random_state=0)
    gm.fit(pts)
    em_fits.append((gm.means_, np.sqrt(gm.covariances_), gm.weights_))

fig, axes = plt.subplots(3, n_test, figsize=(3 * n_test, 9))
row_titles = ["Ground Truth\n(true generating params)", "Classical EM\n(iterative, fit per set)",
              "Set Transformer\n(1 forward pass)"]

for i in range(n_test):
    print("rajat", n_test)
    pts, means, stds, weights, assign = test_tasks[i]

    # row 0: Ground Truth defines the reference color order for this mixture
    plot_clustering(axes[0, i], pts, means, stds, weights, colors)

    # row 1: EM's clusters, reordered to match Ground Truth's
    em_mean, em_std, em_w = em_fits[i]
    em_mean, em_std, em_w = match_to_reference(means, em_mean, em_std, em_w)
    plot_clustering(axes[1, i], pts, em_mean, em_std, em_w, colors)

    # row 2: Set Transformer's clusters, reordered to match Ground Truth's
    st_std_i = st_log_std[i].exp().numpy()
    st_w_i = st_log_w[i].exp().numpy()
    st_mean_i, st_std_i, st_w_i = match_to_reference(means, st_mean[i].numpy(), st_std_i, st_w_i)
    plot_clustering(axes[2, i], pts, st_mean_i, st_std_i, st_w_i, colors)

for r in range(3):
    axes[r, 0].set_ylabel(row_titles[r], fontsize=10)

plt.suptitle("Ground truth vs. classical EM vs. Set Transformer -- colors matched by cluster identity", y=1.01)
plt.tight_layout()
plt.show()
```

There are a few limitations of this model. 
- Let us assume that a single point wants to belong to multiple clusters. This is not possible in current model. An intuitive way to fix is to allow the point do softmax over all possible clusters. However, if a point belongs to `multiple clusters simultanously`, the `one winner behaviour` of softmax won't allow that. Thus the problem reduces to 1-N label assignment problem. 
- Model does not work for no of clusters > K. Of course, taking a large K may help. 
- It is not clear how to train a model with multiple hierarchies of clusters. 
- I can now see motivation of why hinton used em in capsules. But, the slowness of routing killed the idea. So the question is: how to do routing in a fast way, so that it can be used in a deep network. hmm.
